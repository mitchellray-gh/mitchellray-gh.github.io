Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

  1. Describe synonyms as equivalence classes and explain why transitivity needs guarding.
  2. Measure surface-level similarity with edit distance.
  3. Resolve ambiguous terms with a softmax over candidate concepts, and judge how uncertain the result is with entropy.
  4. Expand abbreviations with a noisy-channel (Bayesian) model.
  5. Link raw codes to concepts with pointwise mutual information (PMI).
  6. Measure semantic distance in an ontology graph with path length and information content.

Module 2: Concept Mapping. How synonyms, abbreviations, typos and codes resolve to the same business concept.

2.1The problem: many words, one meaning

In Module 1, we assumed a function C(·) that turns a question into a set of concepts. This module shows how that function can work.

The same business idea appears in many forms:

FormExamples, all meaning Revenue
Synonymsales, turnover, income from sales
Abbreviationrev, REV, Rev.
Typorevnue, reveune
CodeR01, ACCT_4000

The goal is a mapping from every surface form t to a concept c:

L : Surface forms → Concepts L("sales") = L("rev") = L("revnue") = L("R01") = Revenue
L
Lookup rule mapping a written form to its business concept. L("R01") = Revenue.
t
Surface form being interpreted: word, abbreviation, typo, or code. t may be "sales" or "revnue".
c
Candidate business concept. Revenue is the result here.

Show that L takes forms as input and returns concepts as output. "sales" maps to Revenue.
=
Means the two sides have the same result or meaning. Each listed form means Revenue here.
"sales", "rev", "revnue", "R01"
Literal examples: word, abbreviation, typo, and internal code. All map to Revenue.

The difficulty is that the mapping is often ambiguous. “Sales” can mean revenue, units sold, or the sales team. So we need L to output a probability distribution over concepts, not a single answer:

L(t) → P(c | t, context)

given this word and the words around it, how likely is each business concept to be the one meant?

L(t)
Mapping's output for input form t. For "sales", candidates include Revenue and UnitsSold.
t
The exact text being interpreted. t = "sales".
P(c | t, context)
Chance, from 0 to 1, that c is meant given form t and surrounding words; candidate chances sum to 1. "by region" favors Revenue for "sales".
P and |
P means chance; | means "given". P(Revenue | "sales", "by region") is that contextual chance.
c, context
c is a possible meaning; context is the surrounding wording. Revenue is a candidate; "by region" is context.
→
Shows the output is a probability distribution, not one blind guess. Each meaning of "sales" gets its own chance.

People say the same thing in many ways and different things in the same way. The system keeps a ranked list of “what you probably meant” instead of blindly picking the first guess.

It is a mail sorter reading messy handwriting. “Rev”, “revnue” and “R01” are different scrawls, but they all belong in the Revenue pigeonhole. When a scrawl could fit two pigeonholes, the sorter looks at the rest of the envelope for clues.

2.2Equivalence classes

Definition

Define a relation ~ on surface forms: t1 ~ t2 if both refer to the same concept. For ~ to be an equivalence relation, it must be:

PropertyFormalMeaning
Reflexivet ~ tEvery term means itself
Symmetrict1 ~ t2 ⇒ t2 ~ t1Synonymy goes both ways
Transitivet1 ~ t2 ∧ t2 ~ t3 ⇒ t1 ~ t3Synonyms of synonyms are synonyms

An equivalence relation partitions the vocabulary into disjoint classes:

[Revenue] = { revenue, sales, turnover, rev, top line } [Customer] = { customer, client, account holder, cust } [Region] = { region, territory, geo, area }
[Revenue]
Same-meaning group for Revenue. It includes revenue, sales, turnover, rev, and top line.
[Customer]
Same-meaning group for Customer. It includes customer, client, account holder, and cust.
[Region]
Same-meaning group for Region. It includes region, territory, geo, and area.
= and { }
The equals sign defines a group; braces enclose its members. sales belongs to [Revenue].
Listed terms
Each is a surface form for the same concept, not a separate concept. turnover is another form for Revenue.

Every term belongs to exactly one class, and its concept is the class it lives in.

An equivalence class is a “same-meaning bucket”. Every word goes in exactly one bucket, and words in the same bucket can be swapped for each other.

The transitivity trap

Real language is not perfectly transitive. Chaining loose synonyms causes semantic drift:

sales ~ revenue ~ income ~ earnings ~ salary ⇒ sales ~ salary ✗
~
Means "is treated as a synonym of". sales ~ revenue is a strong link.
sales, revenue, income, earnings, salary
Proposed synonym chain; its links become less trustworthy along the way. salary is not a safe synonym for sales.
⇒
Means "therefore" or "the chain would imply". Blind chaining implies sales ~ salary.
✗
Marks the resulting claim as wrong or unsafe. Sales and salary should not be merged.

It is the game of telephone: each person repeats the message almost correctly, yet after five people it is a different sentence.

Fix 1: multiply confidences along a chain. Each link carries a confidence, and the confidence of a chain is the product of its links:

conf(t₁ ~ tₖ) = Πi=1k−1 conf(tᵢ ~ tᵢ₊₁)
conf(x ~ y)
Confidence from 0 to 1 that neighboring terms are synonyms. Link confidences are 0.9, 0.8, 0.6, 0.5.
t₁, tₖ
First and last terms in the chain; subscripts mark their positions. They can be sales and salary.
Π
Multiply all link confidences together. 0.9 × 0.8 × 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.216.
i
Position index moving one link at a time along the chain. i = 1 is sales ~ revenue.
k
Number of terms, so k - 1 is number of links. Five terms give four links.
tᵢ, tᵢ₊₁
Terms at positions i and the next position, forming one adjacent link. The final link is earnings ~ salary.
=
Says total chain confidence equals the product of its links. The four example links give 0.216.

For example, 0.9 × 0.8 × 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.216, which is too weak to trust.

Fix 2: only allow strict synonym edges to merge classes. Weaker relations such as related-to never merge classes. Instead they become distances (see 2.7).

2.3Surface similarity: edit distance

Typos and spelling variants are caught by measuring how many character edits separate two strings.

Levenshtein distance

The minimum number of insertions, deletions or substitutions needed to turn string a into string b. It’s computed with dynamic programming:

lev(i, j) = min { lev(i−1, j) + 1, (delete) lev(i, j−1) + 1, (insert) lev(i−1, j−1) + 1[aᵢ ≠ bⱼ] } (substitute)

fill a grid where each cell is the cheapest way to turn the first i letters of a into the first j letters of b: step down, step right, or step diagonally (free if the letters match).

lev(i, j)
Smallest whole-number edit count to change the first i characters of a into the first j of b. "revnue" to "revenue" takes one edit.
a and b
The two strings being compared. a = "revnue", b = "revenue".
i and j
Character counts into a and b; they index prefixes. i = 3 selects prefix "rev".
min { }
Choose the least costly listed edit option. Matching letters can be kept at no extra cost.
i - 1, j - 1
Previous character positions, used for shorter prefixes. lev(i - 1, j - 1) is the above-left grid cell.
+ 1 and [aᵢ ≠ bⱼ]
One edit is added for an operation; the bracket is 1 for different characters and 0 for a match. The missing e needs one insertion.
delete, insert, substitute
Remove from a, add to a, or replace a character to match b. Insert e to turn "revnue" into "revenue".

Normalized similarity

sim_lex(a, b) = 1 − lev(a, b) / max(|a|, |b|)
sim_lex(a, b)
Spelling closeness from 0 to 1; 1 is identical. "revnue" vs "revenue" scores 0.857.
1 - lev(a, b) / max(|a|, |b|)
Subtract the fraction of the longer string changed by edits from 1. 1 - 1/7 is about 0.857.
lev
Minimum character edits needed between the complete strings. One missing e means lev = 1.
|a|, |b|, max
|a| is a string's character count; max chooses the longer length for normalization. "revenue" has 7 characters.
a and b
The two strings compared. a = "revnue", b = "revenue".

Example: “revnue” vs. “revenue”

One insertion (the missing "e"), so lev = 1 max length = 7 sim_lex = 1 − 1/7 ≈ 0.857
lev, max length, sim_lex
They are the edit count, longer character count, and normalized spelling score. For this example they are 1, 7, and about 0.857.
1, 1/7, 0.857
One edit divided by seven characters is subtracted from perfect score 1. 1 - 1/7 = about 0.857.
−, /, ≈
Subtract; divide to get the changed fraction; ≈ means rounded. 1/7 is about 0.143.

Normalization first. Before comparing, apply cheap transforms such as lower-casing, trimming punctuation and reducing words to their stems (“revenues” → “revenue”). Many matches are exact after normalization.

Edit distance is the number of keystrokes (insert, delete, replace) a spell-checker needs to fix a word. Few keystrokes means probably the same word typed badly.

Edit distance knows nothing about meaning. “cost” and “coast” are 1 edit apart. “revenue” and “turnover” are 7 edits apart (almost every letter of “turnover” must be changed, and one extra letter added). This is why lexical similarity is only one input among several.

Used in the next section: s(t, c) is how closely t matches c’s known aliases, for example the maximum embedding cosine over the class [c]. ctx(c, Q) is how well c fits the other concepts in the question. λ is how much context matters. T is the temperature: a low T makes the distribution sharper and more decisive; a high T makes it flatter and more cautious.

2.4Resolving ambiguity: softmax over concepts

When a term could mean several concepts, we score each candidate and turn the scores into probabilities.

The model

P(c | t, Q) = exp( z(c) / T ) / Σc' exp( z(c') / T ) z(c) = s(t, c) + λ · ctx(c, Q)

score every candidate, exponentiate so that all scores are positive and bigger gaps count for more, then divide by the total so the answers add up to 1.

P(c | t, Q)
Chance from 0 to 1 that concept c is meant for term t in question Q; candidate chances sum to 1. Revenue gets about 0.44 for "sales by region".
P and |
P means probability; | means "given". P(Revenue | "sales", question) is its contextual chance.
exp and e
exp(x) = e^x, with e about 2.718; this converts scores to positive weights. e^1.5 = 4.482.
z(c)
Score for candidate c before conversion to probability. Revenue's score is 1.5.
T
Positive temperature: lower values sharpen the distribution; higher values flatten it. The example uses T = 1.
Σ, c'
Add all candidates' exponentiated scores; the prime marks another candidate. 4.482 + 3.320 + 2.460 = 10.261.
z(c) = s(t,c) + λ · ctx(c,Q)
Combines direct match with weighted context fit. Revenue: 0.9 + 1 × 0.6 = 1.5.
s(t,c), ctx(c,Q)
Direct match score and fit with the question's other words. Revenue has s = 0.9 and ctx = 0.6.
λ
Context tuning weight; raising it gives context more influence. λ = 1 here.
t, c, Q
Observed form, candidate concept, and whole question. Q is "Show sales by region last quarter."

Context coherence

A concept fits better when it commonly appears with the other concepts already in the question:

ctx(c, Q) = Σcⱼ ∈ C(Q) \ {c} w(c, cⱼ)
ctx(c,Q)
Context-fit score for concept c, summing how well it relates to other concepts in question Q. Revenue fits with Region.
Σ
Add each listed relationship score. The resulting sum supports Revenue in "sales by region".
C(Q)
Set of concepts represented in question Q. Region is among the example question's concepts.
c, cⱼ
Candidate and another concept; j labels which other concept is considered. Revenue is c; Region can be c_j.
C(Q) \ {c}
All question concepts except c itself. When c is Revenue, consider Region, not Revenue again.
w(c,cⱼ)
Co-occurrence weight, larger when the two concepts often appear together. Revenue and Region commonly occur together.
Q
The full question providing context. "Show sales by region last quarter."

Here w(c, cj) is how often c and cj appear together in practice. For example, “Revenue” with “Region” is common, while “Sales Team” with “Quarter total” is less common.

Entropy is a “confusion meter”. It is 0 when one answer has all the probability, and at its highest when every answer is equally likely. A normalized entropy of 0.97 means the system is almost guessing.

Temperature is how bold a judge is. At a low T the judge announces the top scorer loudly even if the lead is tiny; at a high T the judge shrugs and says “they all look about the same”.

2.5Abbreviations: the noisy-channel model

Think of an abbreviation as a corrupted message. Someone had a full form f in mind and sent a compressed version a. We want to recover f.

Bayes’ rule

P(f | a) ∝ P(a | f) · P(f) ───────── ──── "could f "how common shorten is f here?" to a?"
P(f | a)
Chance of intended full phrase f given observed abbreviation a. For region context, "NA" is likely North America.
f
Candidate full phrase the writer intended. North America, Not Applicable, or Net Amount.
a
Observed abbreviation. a = "NA".
P(a | f)
Likelihood: how plausible it is that f would be shortened to a; | means "given". P("NA" | North America) = 0.9.
P(f)
Prior chance of f before abbreviation evidence; it can depend on context. North America's prior is 0.85 in a region column.
·
Multiply likelihood and prior to get a candidate's unnormalized weight. 0.9 × 0.85 = 0.765.
∝
Proportional to: weights are later divided by their total to become probabilities. 0.765 / 0.88 is about 0.87.

You get a text message that just says “NA”. You ask two questions: “could a sender abbreviate any of my candidate phrases this way?” (likelihood) and “which of those phrases is this person likely to be talking about right now?” (prior). The second question usually decides it.

“NA”, “N/A”, “Unknown” and empty strings often appear as placeholder values in data. A good mapping keeps them in a separate “missing or unknown” class rather than treating them as a real category.

2.6Codes in the data: pointwise mutual information

Internal codes such as “R01”, “ACCT_4000” or “ST-07” often have no linguistic structure at all. We can only learn what they mean from where they appear.

PMI

Measures whether a code v and a concept c appear together more than chance would predict:

PMI(v, c) = log₂ [ P(v, c) / ( P(v) · P(c) ) ]

compare how often the two really show up together with how often they would meet by pure coincidence. More than coincidence means a link.

PMI(v,c)
Pointwise mutual information: how much code v and concept c co-occur beyond chance. R01 is strongly associated with Region.
v and c
v is a code or observed value; c is a business concept. v = R01; c = Region.
log₂
Base-2 logarithm, producing a score in bits. Ratio above 1 is positive; below 1 is negative. log₂(4) = +2 bits.
P(v,c)
Joint chance that v and c appear together, from 0 to 1. P(R01, Region) = 0.04.
P(v), P(c)
Separate chances of seeing the code and the concept. P(R01)=0.05; P(Region)=0.20.
· and /
Multiply separate chances for expected co-occurrence, then divide joint chance by that expectation. 0.04 / (0.05 × 0.20) = 4.
PMI valueInterpretation
> 0Appear together more than expected: likely related
≈ 0Independent
< 0Appear together less than expected: likely unrelated

If two strangers are both tall and both like jazz, that tells you little. If two people show up at the same tiny jazz club far more often than chance allows, they probably know each other. PMI measures the “more than chance” part.

Worked example

Suppose we examine 1,000 documented field contexts, such as column descriptions, report labels and filters:

  • Code “R01” appears in 50 of them, so P(v) = 0.05.
  • Concept Region appears in 200, so P(Region) = 0.20.
  • They appear together in 40, so P(v, Region) = 0.04.
  • Concept Revenue appears in 300, so P(Revenue) = 0.30.
  • “R01” and Revenue appear together in 5, so P(v, Revenue) = 0.005.
PMI(R01, Region) = log₂( 0.04 / (0.05 × 0.20) ) = log₂( 0.04 / 0.010 ) = log₂ 4 = +2.0 bits PMI(R01, Revenue) = log₂( 0.005 / (0.05 × 0.30) ) = log₂( 0.005 / 0.015 ) = log₂ 0.333 ≈ −1.6 bits
PMI(R01, Region)
Association score of code R01 with Region. 0.04 joint versus 0.010 expected gives log₂(4) = +2.0 bits.
PMI(R01, Revenue)
Association score of R01 with Revenue. 0.005 joint versus 0.015 expected gives about -1.6 bits.
P(v), P(c), P(v,c)
Separate code chance, concept chance, and joint chance. For Region: 0.05, 0.20, and 0.04.
log₂, ×, /
Multiply separate chances, divide joint by expected, then take base-2 log. 0.04 / (0.05 × 0.20) = 4; log₂4 = +2.
+2.0 and -1.6 bits
Positive means more co-occurrence than chance; negative means less. This supports R01 as a region code.
≈
Means the number is rounded. log₂(0.333) is about -1.6.

Conclusion: “R01” is a region code, not a revenue code, even though both start with “R”.

Positive PMI (PPMI). In practice, negative values are noisy when counts are small, so they are often clipped: PPMI = max(0, PMI).

PMI exaggerates relationships for very rare codes. Common remedies are to require a minimum count, or to add a smoothing constant k to every count (P → (count + k) / (N + k·|V|)).

Some concepts aren’t synonyms but are still close. “Gross revenue” and “net revenue” are siblings. “Revenue” and “cost” are both financial metrics, but they are opposites in practice. An ontology graph captures this structure.

Method 2: Information-content similarity (Lin)

The path method treats every edge equally. But two concepts that share a specific ancestor (such as Revenue) are more alike than two that share only a generic ancestor (such as Financial Metric).

Information content: IC(c) = −ln P(c), where P(c) is the probability that a random mention falls under c or any concept below it. Specific concepts are rare, so they have high IC.

sim_Lin(c₁, c₂) = 2 · IC( lcs(c₁, c₂) ) / ( IC(c₁) + IC(c₂) )
sim_Lin(c₁,c₂)

c₁, c₂

IC(c)
Information content, -ln P(c); rarer, more specific concepts have larger values. IC(Revenue) = 1.609.

Lowest common subsumer: most specific ancestor both concepts share. Gross and Net share Revenue.

Why not just use embeddings?

Embedding models learn from which words appear in similar contexts. “Revenue” and “cost” appear in very similar sentences (“Q3 ___ rose 5%”), so their embeddings are often very close. An ontology encodes that they are distinct, even opposite concepts.

cos(φ("revenue"), φ("cost")) ≈ high ← embeddings: "these are alike" sim_Lin(Revenue, Cost) ≈ low ← ontology: "these differ"
cos(φ("revenue"), φ("cost"))

φ(word)
Embedding vector: list of numbers learned from the word's surrounding text. φ("revenue") represents revenue's usage.
cos and high
Cosine compares vector direction; high means the words are used in similar contexts. Revenue and cost appear in similar business sentences.

Ontology-based score using how specific their shared ancestor is. They share broad Financial Metric, so similarity is low.
low, ≈
Low is a qualitative small score; ≈ says the comparison is approximate. The ontology sees Revenue and Cost as distinct.

Embeddings measure relatedness, while ontologies measure sameness. Concept mapping needs both.

Hot and cold are used in nearly the same sentences (“the water is ___”), so a reader of context alone thinks they are alike. A thermometer scale shows they sit at opposite ends. Embeddings are the reader; the ontology is the scale.

Each signal is a witness. Embeddings say “sounds similar”, edit distance says “spelled similarly”, the abbreviation model says “could be a shortening”, PMI says “shows up with this concept”, the ontology says “fits the question”, and the prior says “common anyway”. The weights decide whom to trust for each kind of term.

  1. Compute: what is the normalized Levenshtein similarity between “custmer” and “customer”?One insertion, so lev = 1, and the max length is 8. The similarity is 1 − 1/8 = 0.875.
  2. Compute: three candidate concepts have scores z = (2, 1, 0) with T = 1. What are their softmax probabilities?e² = 7.389, e¹ = 2.718, e⁰ = 1. The sum is 11.107. The probabilities are ≈ (0.665, 0.245, 0.090).
  3. Compute: a code appears in 10% of contexts, a concept in 10%, and both together in 2%. What is the PMI in bits?log₂(0.02 / (0.1 × 0.1)) = log₂(2) = +1 bit. They co-occur twice as often as chance.

Reason: in the “sales” example, what happens to the probabilities if the temperature T is raised from 1 to 10?The scores are divided by 10, giving 0.15, 0.12 and 0.09, so the distribution becomes nearly uniform (≈ 0.34, 0.33, 0.32).   5. Reason: why might two concepts with high embedding similarity still have low Lin similarity?Embeddings capture contextual relatedness: the words appear in similar sentences. Lin captures taxonomic sameness: the concepts share a specific ancestor. Opposites such as revenue and cost are contextually related but taxonomically distinct.

Key takeaways

  • Synonyms form equivalence classes, but transitivity must be confidence-weighted to prevent drift.
  • Edit distance catches typos, noisy-channel Bayes expands abbreviations, and PMI decodes opaque codes.
  • Softmax plus entropy turns ambiguous terms into calibrated probabilities, and the τ / δ thresholds decide when to ask instead of guess.
  • Context-dependent priors do most of the disambiguation work.
  • Embeddings measure relatedness, while ontologies measure sameness. Robust mapping combines both.
