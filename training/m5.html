5.2Matching slots to fields: bipartite matching

The setup

Build a bipartite graph:

  • Left nodes: the template slots, written S = {s1, …, sm}
  • Right nodes: the candidate fields, written F = {f1, …, fn}
  • Edge weights: w(s, f) = how well field f represents slot s

Weights come from the Module 2 signals: embedding similarity between names and descriptions, the lexicon, type compatibility (a measure needs a numeric field, a time slot needs a date field) and curated metadata.

“Bipartite” just means two groups: the things we are looking for (slots) on one side and the things we could use (columns) on the other. Lines connect them, and each line has a score for “how good a fit is this?”.

The objective

Choose a matching M: a set of slot–field pairs in which no slot and no field is used twice.

M* = argmax_M Σ_{(s,f) ∈ M} w(s, f)

choose the one-to-one pairing whose scores add up to the biggest total.

M*
The best valid set of slot-to-field matches; the star means best-scoring.
argmax_M
Choose the matching M that makes the following total as large as possible.
Σ
Add up the weight of every matched pair in M.
(s, f) ∈ M
One slot-field pair that belongs to the matching M.
s
A part of the request that needs a field, such as measure or dimension. Revenue is a measure slot
f
A column in a candidate data source. amount is a candidate revenue field
w(s, f)
A fit score for pairing field f with slot s; larger means a better fit. Revenue to amount scores 0.90

If each weight is a log probability, w(s, f) = log P(f represents s), then maximizing the sum is the same as maximizing the joint probability that all the matches are right. This is the same trick used for join paths in Module 3.

Why greedy fails

Greedy matching takes the single best pair first, then the best remaining pair, and so on. Here is a small case where that goes wrong:

Revenue0.900.80
Refund0.850.10

amount is a generic name, so it fits both concepts. sales_total only fits Revenue.

Greedy: Revenue → amount (0.90), then Refund → sales_total (0.10) total = 1.00 Optimal: Revenue → sales_total (0.80), Refund → amount (0.85) total = 1.65

Greedy grabbed the most attractive pair and left Refund with a nonsense match. The optimal solution gives up a little on Revenue to gain a lot on Refund.

Seating guests at a wedding: if you give the loudest guest their first-choice seat without looking at anyone else, you can end up putting the person who only gets along with that seat’s neighbour at the far end of the room. A good planner looks at the whole table.

The Hungarian algorithm (intuition)

The Hungarian algorithm finds the optimal matching in O(n³) time. The idea:

  1. Turn it into a cost-minimization problem (cost = max weight − weight).
  2. Subtract each row’s minimum, then each column’s minimum, so that zeros mark “best available” choices.
  3. Look for a full set of zeros in which no two share a row or column. If none exists, adjust the costs and repeat.

The result is the globally best assignment, never a locally greedy one.

A relay race of five runners who each have a 75–90% chance of a clean baton pass. Every handover seems safe, but the chance that all five are clean is well under half. One dropped baton ruins the race.

Asking the user to settle a stage raises that stage’s pi to 1. The new overall confidence becomes Conf / pi, so the lowest pi gives the largest gain.

5.5Sanity checks: does the answer look plausible?

Even a well-built plan can produce an implausible number, often because of a join trap (Module 3, section 3.7) or a wrong filter. Sanity checks compare the answer with what we’d expect.

It is the “does that smell right?” check an accountant does before sending a report: is it close to last time’s figure, is it wildly out of the usual range, and do the parts add up?

5.6Ranking and retrieval: two stages

There may be thousands of candidate sources or plans. Scoring each one in depth is too slow, so rankers work in two stages:

Stage 1 (recall): cheap score over everything → keep the top K (e.g. K = 50) Stage 2 (precision): rich score over those K only → final order

Hiring: a quick CV screen over 1,000 applicants picks 50 to interview (Stage 1, cheap and broad). The long interviews only happen with those 50 (Stage 2, rich and slow). If the CV screen drops the best person, no interview can bring them back.

Stage 1: retrieve broadly

  • Embedding nearest-neighbour search with cos(φ(Q), φ(d)) (Module 1), plus a keyword score.
  • Approximate nearest-neighbour methods trade a little accuracy for very large speed-ups.
  • The goal is not to miss anything relevant. It’s measured by recall at K:

5.7Learning to rank

How do we choose the weights θ? We learn them from examples of good and bad rankings. There are three families of objectives.

Show the system many examples where humans said “this one is better than that one”, and nudge the weights until its scores agree. The three families differ in what they ask the system to get right: each score, each pair, or the whole list.

Pointwise: predict each item’s relevance

5.8Diversity: maximal marginal relevance (MMR)

A ranking full of near-duplicates wastes the top positions. Three copies of the same table rank highly, but tell the user nothing new.

The MMR rule

  • λ = 1: pure relevance (no diversity)
  • λ = 0: pure novelty (ignores relevance)

Build the list one item at a time. Each pick balances relevance against similarity to what has already been chosen:

next = argmax_{d ∉ S} [ λ · r(Q, d) − (1 − λ) · max_{s ∈ S} sim(d, s) ]

pick the item that is relevant but not a copy of something already on the list.

5.9Evaluating rankings

How do we know a ranker is good? Compare its output to human relevance judgments.

Precision and recall at k

Precision@k = relevant items in top k / k Recall@k = relevant items in top k / all relevant items
Precision@k
The fraction of the top k results that are relevant, 0 to 1.
Recall@k
The fraction of all relevant results found in the top k, 0 to 1.
relevant items in top k
How many relevant results are among the first k.
k
How many top positions are examined; a whole number.
all relevant items
The total number of relevant results that exist.
/
Divide the relevant count by k (precision) or by all relevant items (recall).

These metrics ignore order within the top k.

Mean reciprocal rank (MRR)

This measures how high the first relevant item appears, averaged over queries:

MRR = (1 / |queries|) · Σ_q 1 / rank of first relevant item for q

Mean reciprocal rank: the average of 1/rank of the first relevant result; 0 to 1.
|queries|
The number of queries; the bars mean count. three queries
Σ_q
Add up one term for every query q.
q
One search question in the test set.

The position, counting from 1, of the first useful result for q.

The reward: rank 1 gives 1, rank 2 gives 1/2, rank 3 gives 1/3.
(1 / |queries|)
Divide by the number of queries to get an average.

Normalized discounted cumulative gain (nDCG)

nDCG uses graded relevance (0 = irrelevant … 3 = perfect) and discounts items lower in the list:

DCG@k = Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ (2^{relᵢ} − 1) / log₂(i + 1) nDCG@k = DCG@k / IDCG@k (IDCG = DCG of the ideal ordering; nDCG ∈ [0, 1])
DCG@k
Discounted cumulative gain through position k; higher when strong results sit near the top.

Add one term for each position i from 1 to k.

Putting the second-best item first costs about 17% of the ideal score. The exponential gain (2rel − 1) means that misplacing the best item hurts most.

nDCG gives big credit for putting great results near the top and shrinking credit for results further down. It then divides by the best score that was possible, so 1.0 means “perfect order”.

5.10Putting it together: the candidate score

For each candidate plan π (sources, joins, field matches, filters), combine everything from this module into one score:

log Score(π) = Σ_{(s,f) ∈ M*} log P(f represents s) ← field matching (5.2) + Σ_values log P(value valid in field) ← value validation (5.3) + Σ_stages log pᵢ ← stage confidence (5.4) + log Consistency(A_π) + log P(correct | checks) ← sanity checks (5.5) + log r(Q, sources of π) ← retrieval rank (5.6–5.7)

Add up a term for each slot-field pair in the best matching M*.

Σ_values
Add up a term for each literal value in the question.

The chance that the value exists in the matched field.

Add up the log confidence of every pipeline stage i.

Read it left to right: add the log evidence for field matching, value validation, stage confidence, sanity checks and retrieval rank; the plan with the highest total is the best candidate.

The candidates are then ordered by score and diversified with MMR (5.8). The leading candidates go to Module 6, which selects the final answer.

Check your understanding

  1. Compute: w(A, x) = 0.6, w(A, y) = 0.5, w(B, x) = 0.55 and w(B, y) = 0.2. What totals do greedy and optimal matching give?Greedy: A → x (0.6), then B → y (0.2), giving 0.80. Optimal: A → y (0.5) and B → x (0.55), giving 1.05.
  2. Compute: three stages have confidences 0.9, 0.8 and 0.5. What is the overall confidence, and what does it become after clarifying the weakest stage?0.9 × 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.36. Clarifying the 0.5 stage gives 0.36 / 0.5 = 0.72.
  3. Compute: what is the pairwise loss when r(d⁺) = r(d⁻)?ln(1 + e0) = ln 2 ≈ 0.693.
  4. Compute: the relevance grades in rank order are [0, 3]. What is nDCG@2?DCG = 0 + 7 / log2 3 ≈ 4.417. IDCG = 7 / 1 + 0 = 7. nDCG = 4.417 / 7 ≈ 0.631.
  5. Compute: for two queries, the first relevant item appears at rank 2 and rank 4. What is the MRR?(1/2 + 1/4) / 2 = 0.375.
  6. Reason: why might each stage look reliable while the overall answer is still uncertain?Confidence multiplies across stages. Five stages that are each about 75–90% reliable can combine to well under 50%, because an error at any stage spoils the final answer.

Key takeaways

  • Field matching is a global assignment problem. The Hungarian algorithm beats greedy picking, and log-probability weights turn it into joint probability.
  • Validate every value against the data, and infer unknown columns with Bayes’ rule. An empty result means a filter is wrong.
  • Confidence compounds: multiply across stages, and ask about the weakest link.
  • Sanity checks are evidence. A failed check can rightly collapse confidence through Bayes’ rule.
  • Retrieval runs in two stages: recall first (errors there are permanent), then precision.
  • Learning to rank should optimize order, through pairwise or listwise objectives.
  • MMR keeps near-duplicates from crowding the top, and nDCG and MRR judge rankings by order, not just membership.
