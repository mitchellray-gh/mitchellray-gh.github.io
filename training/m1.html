1.1The big picture

Every business question asks the same thing: of all the data available, which pieces answer this, and how?

We write that as a single goal: find the answer A that is most probable given the question Q, the data D and our business knowledge 𝒪.

A* = argmax_A P(A | Q, D, O)

out of every possible answer, pick the one that is most likely right, given what was asked, what data exists, and what we know about the business.

A*
The selected answer candidate. For “monthly sales by region,” it could be a table of revenue by month and region.
argmax_A
Choose the A that makes the following score largest; A ranges over possible answers. Compare candidate answer tables and keep the highest-scoring one.
P(…)
A probability or plausibility score from 0 to 1. 0.66 means about 66 chances in 100 under this model.
|
“Given”: judge the left side assuming the items on the right. P(A | Q,D,O) means chance of A given question Q, data D, and business map O.
Q
The user’s question, treated as text. “What were monthly sales by region”?
D
The collection of available data sources. Orders, regional revenue summaries, and employee directory tables.
O
The ontology, a curated map of business concepts and relationships. It can record that Revenue is a financial metric.

This is too hard to solve in one step, so we split it into stages. Each later module covers one stage:

Module 1 · you are hereRelevance
Which sources are related to the question?
→
Module 2Concept mapping
Do “sales”, “rev” and “turnover” mean the same thing?
→
Module 3Implicit relationships
How do customers connect to orders?
→
Module 4Intent and entities
Trend, ranking or lookup? Which “Paris”?
→
Module 5Matching, confidence, ranking
Which field is which concept? How sure are we?
→
Module 6Answer selection
Which candidate answer wins?

Think of a good analyst walking into a library with a question. First they find the right shelves (Module 1), work out what the jargon means (2), figure out how the books cross-reference (3), clarify what the boss actually wants (4), locate the exact page and double-check the numbers (5), and finally decide how to present the answer, or whether to ask a follow-up (6). This system does the same steps, with probabilities instead of gut feel.

1.2The building blocks (notation)

The query

Q is the raw question, for example “What were monthly sales by region?”

q = φ(Q) ∈ ℝd is its embedding: a list of d numbers that captures its meaning.

C is the set of business concepts, such as Revenue, Customer, Region and Month.

R is the set of typed relationships between them, such as Revenue is-a Financial Metric or Order belongs-to Customer.

An embedding is a “meaning address”. φ places every piece of text at a point in a very high-dimensional space so that texts with similar meanings land close together, even when they share no words. “Turnover” and “revenue” end up as neighbours.

Intuition: φ is a function that places text in a “meaning space”. Texts with similar meanings land close together, even when they share no words.

The data sources

D = {d1, d2, …, dn} is the collection of tables, reports and documents. Each source is a bundle of facts about itself:

d_i = (name, description, schema, sample values, usage metadata)
d_i
The i-th data source; i is an integer index counting sources. d_2 is regional_revenue_summary in the worked example.
=
“Is represented by” or “has these parts.” The source is described by the fields on the right.
name
A short identifier for the source. orders_fact.
description
Plain-language explanation of source contents. “Line-level orders with dates and amounts.”
schema
The names and types of the source’s fields. Revenue and Month columns.
sample values
Example values illustrating fields. A Region value such as “West.”
usage metadata
Recorded details about source use, such as its use count. 25 uses is illustrative.

The ontology (business knowledge)

O = (C, R)
O
The ontology: a curated business map. It contains concepts and their links.
C
The set of business concepts in the map. Revenue, Customer, Region, and Month.
R
The set of typed relationships among concepts. Order belongs-to Customer.
( , )
Parentheses group the components making up O. O is made from C and R.

A curated map of the business: its concepts and how they relate.

The lexicon

L : Words → C maps everyday wording to concepts:

L("sales") = L("revenue") = L("rev") = Revenue
L(…)
The lexicon function: a word or phrase goes in, its mapped business concept comes out. L(“sales”) returns Revenue.
“sales”, “revenue”, “rev”
Text inputs to the lexicon; “rev” is an abbreviation. All map to Revenue here.
=
“Maps to the same result as.” Each input produces Revenue.
Revenue
The returned business concept, not a raw word. Income earned from sales.

The concept function

C(·) returns the set of concepts that a query or source is about:

C("monthly sales by region") = { Revenue, Month, Region }
C(…)
The concept function: query or source goes in, its set of business concepts comes out. C(“monthly sales by region”) returns three concepts.
“monthly sales by region”
The question text supplied as input. It asks for Revenue, Month, and Region.
=
“Returns” or “is represented by.” The function result is the set on the right.
{ … }
Curly braces mean a set, an unordered collection without duplicates. This set has three concepts.
Revenue, Month, Region
Business concepts identified in the question. Revenue is the measure; Month and Region are groupings.

1.3Three signals of relevance

There are three independent ways a source can “look related” to a question. Each signal catches cases the others miss.

1 · Semantic

Meaning. Cosine of embeddings.

+ catches paraphrases
− confuses related-but-different ideas (revenue vs. cost)

2 · Lexical

Exact words. BM25.

+ precise on codes and names (“SKU-4471”)
− blind to synonyms

3 · Concept

Business meaning. Coverage of C(Q).

+ grounded in curated knowledge
− only as good as the ontology

Signal 1: Semantic similarity (meaning)

Measure the angle between the two meaning-vectors with cosine similarity:

cos(q, d) = (q · d) / (‖q‖ · ‖d‖) range: −1 to 1
cos(…)
Cosine similarity takes two same-length number vectors and returns a score from -1 to 1; closer to 1 means more alike in direction. q=(1,1,0) and d=(1,0,1) give 0.5.
q
The question embedding, a vector of numbers representing its meaning. q=(1,1,0) in the mini example.
d
The source embedding, with the same coordinate count as q. d=(1,0,1) in the mini example.
·
Dot product: multiply matching coordinates and add the products. q·d=1×1+1×0+0×1=1.
‖q‖, ‖d‖
The vector lengths (norms), calculated from their squared coordinates. Both example vectors have length √2.
/
Divide the dot product by the product of vector lengths. 1/(√2×√2)=0.5.
range: −1 to 1
Allowed cosine-score range: -1 opposite, 0 perpendicular, 1 same direction. The example score 0.5 is between unrelated and same-direction.

Mini example

q = (1, 1, 0) and d = (1, 0, 1).

Dot product: 1·1 + 1·0 + 0·1 = 1 Norms: ‖q‖ = √2 and ‖d‖ = √2, so the product is 2 cos = 1 / 2 = 0.5, which is moderately similar
q, d
Three-coordinate question and source vectors; each coordinate is one numeric part of an embedding. q=(1,1,0), d=(1,0,1).
1, 0
Coordinate values: 1 contributes to a match; 0 contributes nothing when multiplied. Coordinate products are 1, 0, and 0.
·
Multiply corresponding coordinates to form each product. First coordinate: 1×1=1.
+
Add coordinate products to get the dot product. 1+0+0=1.
=
“Has the value.” The dot product is 1.
‖q‖, ‖d‖
Lengths of q and d. Each is √2.
√2
Square root of 2, about 1.414. √2×√2=2.
×
Multiply the two lengths for the cosine denominator. About 1.414×1.414=2.
/
Divide the dot product by the denominator. 1/2=0.5.
0.5
The resulting direction-based cosine similarity. The vectors are moderately similar.

Strength: catches paraphrases (“turnover” ≈ “revenue”). Weakness: can confuse things that are related but different (“revenue” vs. “cost”).

Cosine ignores how long the vectors are and only asks “are they pointing the same way?” Set q = (1, 1, 0) and d = (1, 0, 1) above to reproduce the 0.5 from the example. Then make d a multiple of q: the cosine jumps to 1.

Signal 2: Lexical overlap (exact words)

BM25 is a classic keyword score. It rewards words that are shared and rare, and it adjusts for document length:

BM25(Q, d) = Σ_{t ∈ Q} IDF(t) · [ f(t,d) · (k1 + 1) ] / [ f(t,d) + k1 · (1 − b + b · |d| / avg|d|) ] IDF(t) = log( (N − n_t + 0.5) / (n_t + 0.5) + 1 )
BM25(Q,d)
Keyword-match score for question Q and source d; higher means stronger exact-word evidence. It rewards rare matching terms and adjusts repeats and source length.
Σ_{t ∈ Q}
Add the contribution for every question term t. For the sales question, add its term scores.
Q
The question’s words or terms. “monthly sales by region.”
t
One term being scored, drawn from Q. “region” is one term.
IDF(t)
Inverse document frequency: rarer terms receive more weight. A rare code such as SKU-4471 can count more than “the.”
f(t,d)
Count of term t in source d; a nonnegative whole number. If “region” occurs 3 times, f(region,d)=3.
k1
Positive tuning constant controlling diminishing benefit from repeats; typical value about 1.2. With k1=1.2, the tenth repeat adds less than the first.
b
Length-adjustment tuning constant from 0 to 1; typical value about 0.75. Increasing b strengthens the adjustment for long sources.
|d|
Length of source d, measured in indexed terms. A source with 100 terms has |d|=100.
avg|d|
Average indexed length of all sources. 80 terms is illustrative.
f(t,d)·(k1+1)
The frequency numerator, scaled by k1. More occurrences initially increase the term score.
1−b+b·|d|/avg|d|
Length factor comparing d’s length with the collection average. If |d|=avg|d|, this factor is 1.
N, n_t
N is total source count; n_t counts sources containing t. For 3 sources, if “region” is in 2, N=3 and n_region=2.
0.5
Smoothing constant avoiding zero counts in IDF. It is added to both counts in the ratio.
log(…)
Logarithm compresses the frequency ratio. It keeps IDF growth manageable.
+, −, ·, /
Add, subtract, multiply, and divide as grouped by brackets. Adjust a term’s count for length before adding its score.
  • f(t, d) is how often term t appears in d.
  • nt is the number of sources that contain t, and N is the total number of sources.
  • k1 ≈ 1.2 and b ≈ 0.75 are tuning constants.

Strength: precise on exact codes and names (“SKU-4471”, “EMEA”). Weakness: blind to synonyms.

IDF says a rare word is worth more than a common one: matching “EMEA” is a strong clue, matching “the” is none. The k1 term means the tenth repeat of a word adds far less than the first. The b term stops long documents winning just by containing more words.

Signal 3: Concept coverage (business meaning)

What fraction of the question’s concepts does the source cover?

coverage(Q, d) = |C(Q) ∩ C(d)| / |C(Q)|
coverage(Q,d)
Fraction of question concepts covered by source d; value 0 to 1. d_2 covers 3 of 3 concepts, so coverage is 1.
C(Q), C(d)
Sets of business concepts in the question and source. C(Q)={Revenue,Month,Region}; d_1 has Revenue and Month.
∩
Set intersection: retain items found in both sets. C(Q)∩C(d_1)={Revenue,Month}.
|…|
Set size: count the items between the bars. The intersection has size 2 and |C(Q)|=3.
/
Divide concepts covered by concepts requested. 2/3 is about 0.667.

Strength: grounded in curated business knowledge. Weakness: only as good as the ontology.

Combining them

rel(Q, d) = α · cos(q, d) + β · BM25_norm(Q, d) + γ · coverage(Q, d) with α + β + γ = 1, all ≥ 0
rel(Q,d)
Combined relevance score for question Q and source d. d_1 scores 0.640.
α, β, γ
Nonnegative weights for semantic, keyword, and concept-coverage signals. The example uses 0.5, 0.2, and 0.3.
cos(q,d)
Semantic similarity of the question and source vectors, from -1 to 1. d_1 has 0.72.
BM25_norm(Q,d)
Keyword score rescaled to a comparable normalized value. d_1 has 0.40.
coverage(Q,d)
Fraction of required concepts covered, from 0 to 1. d_1 covers 2 of 3, about 0.667.
·, +
Multiply each signal by its weight, then add. 0.5×0.72+0.2×0.40+0.3×0.667=0.640.
α+β+γ=1
Weights sum to 1 and share the influence across signals. 0.5+0.2+0.3=1.
all ≥ 0
Every weight must be zero or positive. No signal receives a negative weight.

The weights say how much to trust each signal. They can be set by hand or learned from examples of good and bad matches.

Each one has blind spots. A weighted mix is more robust than any single signal, much like averaging several noisy measurements.

1.4Adding trust with Bayes’ rule

Being related isn’t enough. A deprecated draft table can look just as related as a certified report. We add a prior: a belief about how trustworthy each source is before we even look at the question.

Bayes’ rule applied to sources

P(d | Q) ∝ P(Q | d) · P(d) ------- ---- "fit" "trust"
P(d | Q)
Probability assigned to source d after considering Q; after normalization values are 0 to 1. d_2 is about 0.66 in the example.
|
“Given”: the left side is assessed assuming the right side. P(Q|d) measures how well d fits Q.
∝
“Is proportional to”: use the right side before scaling candidate scores to sum to 1. Multiply fit by trust, then normalize.
P(Q | d)
Likelihood or fit of source d for question Q, approximated by rel(Q,d). d_2 has relevance 0.905.
·
Multiply fit by the source’s prior trust. 0.905×0.5=0.4525 for d_2.
P(d)
Prior trust assigned to source d before checking this question. Certified d_2 has prior 0.5.

Here P(Q | d) is approximated by rel(Q, d).

A model for the prior

P(d) ∝ exp( w1 · curated + w2 · log(1 + usage) − w3 · staleness − w4 · deprecated )
P(d)
Pre-question trust prior for d; exp makes its unnormalized value positive, then candidates can be normalized. A certified source can get a higher prior than a deprecated draft.
∝
“Is proportional to”; scale positive scores so priors sum to 1. d_2 receives prior 0.5 in the example.
exp(…)
Exponential function, e raised to the score in parentheses; output is positive. A larger inside score gives a larger prior.
w1, w2, w3, w4
Tuning weights controlling how strongly each trust feature affects the score. Numeric settings are not given; 0.5 is illustrative only.
curated
Feature for human validation, often recorded as 0 or 1. Certified d_2 is curated.
usage
Nonnegative count of source use. 100 uses is illustrative.
log(1+usage)
Natural logarithm of one plus use count; increases with diminishing returns. 0 to 100 uses matters more than 10,000 to 10,100.
staleness
Nonnegative measure of how out-of-date a source is. A larger value lowers trust.
deprecated
Feature indicating source retirement, often 0 or 1. A deprecated source receives a penalty.
+, −, ·
Multiply weights by features, add positive signals, and subtract penalties. Curation can raise trust; staleness can lower it.
FeatureEffectReason
curated / certified↑Humans validated it
log(1 + usage)↑ (with diminishing returns)Popular sources are battle-tested
staleness↓Old data may mislead
deprecated↓↓Explicitly retired

Going from 0 to 100 uses is a strong signal. Going from 10,000 to 10,100 adds almost nothing. The log captures these diminishing returns.

It keeps the prior positive and turns additive evidence into multiplicative odds, the same form as logistic regression.

1.6When one source isn’t enough: set cover

Some questions span several concepts that no single source holds. Change the question to “monthly sales by region and product category”:

C(Q) = { Revenue, Month, Region, ProductCategory }
C(Q)
Set of concepts needed to answer question Q. Sales by month and region needs Revenue, Month, Region.
Revenue, Month, Region, ProductCategory
The four distinct concepts in the expanded request. ProductCategory is added by “and product category.”
{ … }
A set of distinct items. This set contains four concepts.
=
“Is represented by.” The set has the four listed members.

If no source covers all four, we need the smallest set of sources whose combined coverage includes every needed concept:

S* = argmin_{S ⊆ D} |S| subject to C(Q) ⊆ ∪_{d ∈ S} C(d)
S*
Smallest selected set of sources covering every required concept; * marks the best set. The solution is {d_2,d_4}.
argmin
Choose S that makes the following quantity smallest. Prefer two sources over three if both fully cover the concepts.
S ⊆ D
S must be a subset of available source collection D. Choose d_2 and d_4 from D.
|S|
Number of sources in S. For {d_2,d_4}, |S|=2.
subject to
“Under the condition that”; the chosen set must satisfy what follows. All requested concepts must be covered.
C(Q)
Concept set requested by Q. Revenue, Month, Region, ProductCategory.
⊆
“Is contained in”: each item on the left must appear on the right. Every requested concept must be covered by chosen sources.
∪_{d ∈ S} C(d)
Union of concept sets C(d) for every chosen source d. d_2 covers Revenue, Month, Region; d_4 covers ProductCategory.
d, S, D
d is one source, S is chosen sources, D is all available sources. d_2 and d_4 are selected from D into S.

This is the classic set-cover problem. Finding the exact solution is NP-hard, but a greedy approach works well in practice:

Greedy set cover: U ← C(Q) (concepts still needed) S ← ∅ while U ≠ ∅: d* ← argmax_d |C(d) ∩ U| · P(d | Q) (most new coverage, weighted by trust) S ← S ∪ {d*} U ← U \ C(d*) return S
U
Concepts still needed at the current step. Initially Revenue, Month, Region, ProductCategory.
C(Q)
All concepts required by question Q. The expanded question contains four concepts.
S
Sources chosen so far. After the first step S contains d_2.
←
Assignment arrow: set left-hand variable to right-hand value. U←C(Q) starts with all required concepts.
∅
Empty set, containing no items. S starts empty.
while U ≠ ∅
Repeat while U is not empty. Stop when all needed concepts are covered.
d*
Source selected as best at this step. First d_2, then d_4.
argmax_d
Choose source d that makes the following score largest. Favor new coverage and trust.
C(d)∩U
Concepts in d that are still needed. After d_2, d_4 newly covers {ProductCategory}.
|C(d)∩U|
Count of newly covered concepts. d_4 adds one still-needed concept.
P(d | Q)
Trust/probability score for source d given Q. The widget uses 0.66 for d_2 and 0.55 for d_4.
·
Multiply new-concept count by trust. One new concept with trust 0.55 scores 0.55.
S∪{d*}
Add the selected source to S; braces make a one-item set and cup combines sets. After selecting d_2, S={d_2}.
U \ C(d*)
Remove from U concepts covered by selected d*; \ means set difference. After d_2, only ProductCategory remains.
return S
Finish by providing the selected source set. The final set is {d_2,d_4}.
Q, d
Q is the question; d is one available source. Q asks for sales by month, region, and product category.

Applied

  1. Pick d2, which covers {Revenue, Month, Region}. Still needed: {ProductCategory}.
  2. Pick d4 product_dim, which covers {ProductCategory}. Still needed: ∅.
  3. S* = {d2, d4}. How to connect them is the subject of Module 3.

Greedy set cover is within a factor of ln|C(Q)| + 1 of the optimal solution. For a 4-concept question, that means at most about 2.4× as many sources as the best possible answer, and usually far fewer.

It is packing for a trip with the fewest bags: at each step grab the bag that holds the most still-unpacked items (preferring bags you trust), then repeat until everything is packed.

  1. Compute: given q = (2, 0, 1) and d = (1, 1, 1), what is cos(q, d)?q·d = 2 + 0 + 1 = 3; ‖q‖ = √5 ≈ 2.236; ‖d‖ = √3 ≈ 1.732; cos ≈ 3 / 3.873 ≈ 0.775
  2. Reason: two sources have identical relevance, but one is certified and the other is deprecated. Which part of the formula separates them?The prior P(d): the curated (+) and deprecated (−) terms in the exponent.
  3. Apply: if γ = 0, what kind of mistake becomes more likely?Sources that sound right but don’t hold the needed business concepts get selected. In other words, there’s no check against the ontology.
  4. Extend: in the worked example, raise d1’s prior to 0.6 and lower d2’s to 0.2, keeping d3 at 0.2. Does the ranking change?d1: 0.640 × 0.6 = 0.384. d2: 0.905 × 0.2 = 0.181. Yes, d1 now ranks first. Priors can override relevance, which is why they need careful calibration. (Verify with the calculator above.)

Key takeaways

  • A question and its sources become vectors, word sets and concept sets, and relevance compares all three.
  • Hybrid scoring balances meaning, exact wording and business concepts.
  • Bayes’ rule turns “looks related” into “is related and trustworthy”.
  • Set cover decides how many sources a question needs.
