Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

  1. Classify a question’s intent with Bayesian and softmax models, including questions with more than one intent.
  2. Map an intent to a structural template with slots to fill.
  3. Choose the right aggregation operator from the user’s words, and avoid the average-of-averages trap.
  4. Resolve relative time expressions into exact intervals.
  5. Detect mentions in a question and tag them by type.
  6. Score and link each mention to a specific entity, and resolve several mentions jointly.
  7. Decide when to abstain (NIL) and check whether confidence is calibrated.

Define a finite set of intents:

K = { lookup, count, aggregate, trend, compare, rank, distribution, explain }

The finite set of answer intents the classifier can choose from. It contains the eight choices below.

Single-label vs. multi-label

Some questions carry more than one intent. “Why did revenue drop last month compared to the month before?” is both explain and compare.

  • Single-label: pick one intent, so probabilities sum to 1. Use softmax.
  • Multi-label: each intent is an independent yes/no, so probabilities don’t sum to 1. Use one sigmoid per intent.
Single: P(k | Q) = softmax_k( W_k · q + b_k ) Multi: P(k ∈ Q) = σ( W_k · q + b_k ) include intent k if P > 0.5
Single / Multi
Single-label chooses one intent; multi-label can include independent intents. A revenue-drop question can be both explain and compare.
P(…)
The chance, from 0 to 1, that the statement is true. P(k | Q) is the chance of intent k for question Q.
k
One intent from K. For “top 5 regions by revenue,” k can be rank.
Q
The user's full question, as text or its numeric representation. “top 5 regions by revenue.”
softmax_k(…)
Turns all intent scores into competing probabilities summing to 1; k selects an intent's score. The highest-probability intent is selected.
W_k
The learned weight vector for intent k; its entries show how question features affect its score. Rank weights respond to features like “top.”
·
Dot product: multiply matching vector entries and add the products. W_k · q combines rank weights and question features.
q
A vector of numeric features representing the question. It represents “top 5 regions by revenue.”
b_k
A learned baseline score for intent k, added to its feature score. It shifts the rank score before probabilities are calculated.
σ(…)
Sigmoid: converts one score to an independent probability from 0 to 1. Each intent gets its own inclusion chance.
σ(…)
Means “is one of” or “belongs to.” k ∈ Q means intent k is included for question Q.
> 0.5
Include a multi-label intent only when its probability is over one half. A probability of 0.6 includes it.

Softmax is a single-choice exam: the options compete for one share of 100%. Sigmoids are a tick-all-that-apply form: every box gets its own independent chance.

4.3Classifying intent: a Bayesian view

The simplest interpretable classifier is naive Bayes over cue words:

P(k | Q) ∝ P(k) · Π_{t ∈ Q} P(t | k)

how likely is each intent before reading the words (the prior), multiplied by how typical each cue word is for that intent.

P(k | Q)
The posterior chance of intent k after seeing Q; the bar means “given.” The worked example gives P(rank | Q) ≈ 0.896.
∝
Means “is proportional to”: multiply pieces, then normalize them to probabilities. The worked example's products total 0.0201.
P(k)
The prior chance of intent k before reading cue words. The rank prior is 0.30.
·
Multiply the prior by the cue-word evidence. 0.30 “ 0.30 ” 0.20 = 0.0180 for rank.
Π_{t ∈ Q}
Multiply one likelihood for every cue word t in question Q. The question has cues “top” and “by.”
t
A cue word found in the question. “top” is a cue for rank.
P(t | k)
The chance of seeing cue word t when intent k is true. P(“top” | rank) = 0.30.

The “naive” assumption is that cue words are independent given the intent. That is false in general, but often good enough to rank intents correctly.

Worked example

Question: “top 5 regions by revenue”. The cue words are “top” and “by”.

Intent kPrior P(k)P(“top” | k)P(“by” | k)Product

trend0.300.020.150.30 × 0.02 × 0.15 = 0.0009

The normalizer is 0.0180 + 0.0009 + 0.0012 = 0.0201.

P(rank | Q) = 0.0180 / 0.0201 ≈ 0.896 P(trend | Q) = 0.0009 / 0.0201 ≈ 0.045 P(lookup | Q) = 0.0012 / 0.0201 ≈ 0.060
P(k | Q)
The normalized chance of intent k after seeing question Q. Rank has probability about 0.896.
rank, trend, lookup
The three candidate intents in this calculation. Their products are 0.0180, 0.0009, and 0.0012.
0.0180, 0.0009, 0.0012
The cue-word products for rank, trend, and lookup. They sum to 0.0201.
/ 0.0201
Divide each product by the sum of all products to make probabilities. 0.0180 / 0.0201 ≈ 0.896.
≈
Means “approximately”; the results are rounded. 0.896 + 0.045 + 0.060 is about 1.

Rank wins decisively, driven mostly by “top”. The word “by” appears in many intents, so it carries little information.

Information per cue word. A word’s usefulness is how much it shifts the odds between intents: log [ P(t | k1) / P(t | k2) ]. For “top”, rank vs. trend gives log(0.30 / 0.02) ≈ 2.7 nats, a strong cue. For “by”, it gives log(0.20 / 0.15) ≈ 0.29 nats, a weak one.

ln(15) = 2.708 and ln(4/3) = 0.288, matching the module’s 2.7 and 0.29. The widget prints the same numbers per selected word.

A cue word is useful only if it is much more common in one intent than another. “Top” is a loud signal for ranking; “by” shows up everywhere, so it is nearly noise.

Like a doctor weighing symptoms: “fever” points firmly to a few illnesses, while “feels tired” fits nearly all of them and barely moves the diagnosis.

Beyond word counts

Modern classifiers replace the cue-word table with an embedding q = φ(Q), so paraphrases such as “highest-performing”, “leading” and “best” land near “top” automatically. The Bayesian structure, a prior times evidence, stays the same.

Each intent implies a template: a skeleton with slots that the rest of the pipeline must fill.

template(intent)
The slots required to answer a question with that intent. Rank needs a measure, dimension, direction, N, and filters.

An intent asking for an ordered list. “Top 5 stores by revenue.”

The numeric quantity to calculate or order by. Revenue.
dimension
The kind of item being grouped or ranked. Store.
order direction
Whether values are sorted high-to-low or low-to-high. “Top” means descending revenue.
N
The number of results requested. The question asks for 5 stores.

Conditions limiting included records or items. Paris and last quarter.
trend

A template is a paper form. Choosing the intent picks the form (“ranking request”); the rest of the pipeline fills in each blank. An unfilled required blank means the form cannot be submitted.

Slot completeness

complete(template) = 1 ⇔ every required slot is filled with confidence ≥ τ
complete(…)
A check returning 1 when all required slots are filled, and 0 otherwise. Paris and last quarter still need their filters resolved.
template
The fields required for the chosen intent. The rank template includes N = 5.
= 1
Means the completeness check passes. It passes only when every required slot is filled.
⇔
Means “if and only if”: both statements have the same truth value. The check passes exactly when the condition holds.
confidence
A score from 0 to 1 for how sure the system is that a slot value is right. A value must meet threshold τ.
τ
The minimum acceptable confidence threshold. A slot at or above τ counts as filled.

When a required slot is empty, fill it with a prior if it’s low-risk (Module 3, section 3.9), or ask the user.

The “explain” template is a decomposition

For “why did X change?”, the answer splits the total change into contributions from each segment s:

ΔX = X₁ − X₀ = Σ_s (X₁,s − X₀,s) = Σ_s Δ_s share_s = Δ_s / ΔX ← fraction of the change caused by segment s
ΔX
The total change in measure X between two periods, in X's units. Revenue fell by $100k, so ΔX = −$100k.
X
The measured quantity, here revenue. The example uses thousands of dollars.
X₁ − X₀
Later-period value minus earlier-period value. For a $100k fall, this difference is −$100k.
Δ_s
Add the contribution for every segment s. North and the other regions together explain the change.
s
An index naming one segment, such as a region. s can be North.
X₁,s − X₀,s
The change in X for segment s between the later and earlier periods. North contributes −$80k.
Σ_s
The contribution to the total change from segment s. Δ_North = −$80k.
share_s
The fraction of total change attributable to segment s; it has no units. North explains 80% of the $100k drop.
/
Division expresses a segment contribution as a fraction of the total. (−$80k) / (−$100k) = 0.8.

Example: revenue fell by $100k. The North region accounts for −$80k, and the rest is spread thinly, so North’s share is 80% of the drop.

“Why did it drop?” becomes “which pieces fell, and how much of the total fall does each piece explain?” The shares add up to 100%.

4.5Choosing the aggregation operator

The operator comes from the modifier words in the question, not from the column name. A column called avg_price still needs SUM when the user asks for “total”.

op* = argmax_{op} P(op | modifier words in Q)
op*
The operator chosen as most likely; the star marks the selected answer. For “how many customers,” choose COUNT DISTINCT customer.
argmax
Choose the candidate with the greatest value. Pick the operator that best matches the wording.
op
One candidate aggregation operator, such as SUM, AVG, MAX, MIN, or COUNT. “Total” makes SUM a candidate.
P(op | modifier words in Q)
The chance operator op is right given the modifier words in question Q. “Average” favors AVG; “total” favors SUM.
|
Means “given” or “knowing” in a probability. The operator is selected knowing the user's words.
Modifier cueOperator
“total”, “overall”, “sum of”SUM
“average”, “mean”, “typical”, “per”AVG (weighted, see below)
“highest”, “largest”, “peak”, “maximum”MAX
“lowest”, “smallest”, “minimum”MIN
“how many [entities]”COUNT DISTINCT entity
“how many [records / transactions]”COUNT rows
“median”, “middle”PERCENTILE 0.5

Pitfall 1: rows vs. entities

“How many customers placed orders?” There are 10 order rows from 4 distinct customers.

COUNT(rows) = 10 ✗ (counts orders) COUNT(DISTINCT customer) = 4 ✓ (counts customers)
COUNT(…)
Counts the items named inside the parentheses. COUNT(rows) gives 10 order records.
rows
Individual records; one customer can appear on several rows. There are 10 order rows.
DISTINCT
Count each different value once, even if repeated. Four different customers placed the 10 orders.
customer
The customer identifier whose unique values are counted. COUNT(DISTINCT customer) = 4.
= 10 / = 4
The first count is order rows; the second is unique customers. The question asks for customers, so 4 is the answer.

The noun after “how many” decides which entity is counted.

Counting people in a shop by counting receipts: someone who bought four times gets counted four times. Counting distinct customers is counting faces, not receipts.

Pitfall 2: average of averages

RegionOrdersAverage order value
A10$100
B90$200
Naive: (100 + 200) / 2 = $150 ✗ Weighted: (10 × 100 + 90 × 200) / (10 + 90) = (1,000 + 18,000) / 100 = $190 ✓
Naive
The unweighted mean, giving both region averages equal influence. ($100 + $200) / 2 = $150.
Weighted
Multiply each average by its order count, add those totals, then divide by all orders. The correct overall average is $190.
100, 200
Average order values in regions A and B, in dollars per order. A averages $100; B averages $200.
10, 90
The numbers of orders in regions A and B. B's 90 orders give it nine times A's weight.
×
Multiply a group's average by its order count to get that group's total value. 90 × $200 = $18,000.
+
Add the group values or averages as shown. $1,000 + $18,000 = $19,000 total.
/
Divide by 2 groups for the naive mean, or by 100 orders for the weighted mean. $19,000 / 100 = $190 per order.
2 / 100
Two groups in the naive calculation; 100 total orders in the weighted calculation. 10 + 90 = 100.

In general:

true average = Σ_g n_g · avg_g / Σ_g n_g (weight each group by its size)
true average
The overall mean across individual items, weighted by group size. The correct average is $190 per order.
Σ_g
Add the indicated quantity over every group g. The groups are A and B.
g
An index for one group. A has 10 orders; B has 90.
n_g
The count of items in group g, here orders. n_A = 10 and n_B = 90.
avg_g
The average value within group g, in dollars per order. avg_A = $100; avg_B = $200.
·
Multiply group size by its average to recover that group's total value. 90 × $200 = $18,000 for B.
/
Divide summed group values by summed group counts. $19,000 / 100 orders = $190 per order.

Region B has nine times as many orders, so it must count nine times as much. A plain mean of the two averages pretends both regions are the same size.

Rule: averages, ratios and percentages are not additive. Re-compute them from their parts, Σ numerator / Σ denominator, never by averaging pre-computed averages.

4.6Resolving time expressions

Relative phrases need an anchor date (today) and a calendar convention to become exact intervals. We use half-open intervals [start, end), so adjacent periods never overlap or leave gaps.

Suppose today is 2026-10-21 and the calendar is a standard calendar year:

PhraseInterval
“last quarter”[2026-07-01, 2026-10-01): Q3 2026
“last month”[2026-09-01, 2026-10-01)
“year to date”[2026-01-01, 2026-10-22)
“last 30 days”[2026-09-21, 2026-10-21), or up to 10-22 if today counts
“same quarter last year”[2025-07-01, 2025-10-01)

Half-open intervals work like floor numbering: “1st floor up to, but not including, the 2nd” means two neighbouring floors never share a ceiling slab or leave a gap.

Ambiguity is real

These are latent variables, as in Module 3:

interval* = argmax_I P(I | phrase, convention, intent)
interval*
The selected time interval that best matches the phrase and assumptions. On 2026-10-21, calendar “last quarter” is [2026-07-01, 2026-10-01).
argmax_I
Choose candidate interval I with the highest probability. Calendar and fiscal rules can give different quarters.
P(I | phrase, convention, intent)
The chance interval I is right given the wording, calendar rules, and requested answer type. A trend usually prefers complete periods.
I
A candidate interval with a start and end date. [2026-07-01, 2026-10-01).
|
Means “given” or “knowing” in a probability. The phrase and calendar convention are evidence.
  • Fiscal vs. calendar: if the fiscal year starts on 1 February, “last quarter” is a different interval.
  • Complete vs. partial: does “this month” include today’s incomplete data?
  • Rolling vs. calendar: “last 3 months” can mean the previous three full months or the trailing 90 days.

A trend intent prefers complete periods, so the last partial month doesn’t create a fake drop. A lookup of “this month so far” accepts a partial period.

With the fiscal year starting 1 February, today 2026-10-21 falls in the quarter Aug–Oct, so “last quarter” becomes [2026-05-01, 2026-08-01) instead of the calendar answer [2026-07-01, 2026-10-01).

Fake-drop trap: comparing a partial current period with a full previous one makes almost every metric look like it fell. A month that is 21 of 31 days complete shows about a 32% “drop” even at a perfectly steady rate (1 − 21/31 ≈ 0.32).

4.8Linking mentions to entities

Each detected mention m must resolve to a concrete entity e from a set of candidates E(m).

Step 1: Generate candidates

Gather plausible entities using the lexical and semantic matching from Module 2: exact matches, near spellings and known aliases. Keep the top few (|E(m)| ≈ 5–20).

Step 2: Score each candidate

score(m, e) = θ₁ · sim_lex(m, e) ← spelling match + θ₂ · P(e | m) ← prior: how often m refers to e + θ₃ · type_fit(e, slot) ← does e have the type the template expects? + θ₄ · coherence(e, Q) ← fit with the other concepts in the question
score(m, e)
A numeric fit score for linking mention m to candidate entity e; higher is better. Paris, France scores 4.4.
m
A span of question words that refers to something. The mention is “Paris.”
e
One possible real-world entity for mention m. Paris, France is a candidate.
θ₁…θ₄
Weights controlling how much each scoring feature matters; increasing a weight emphasizes that feature. θ = (1, 1, 2, 1), so type fit counts double.
sim_lex(m, e)
A 0-to-1 spelling or wording similarity between mention and candidate. Both Paris city candidates score 1.0.
P(e | m)
The prior chance that mention m refers to entity e, before using other question context. Paris, France has prior 0.6.
type_fit(e, slot)
A 0-to-1 match between candidate's kind and the slot's expected kind. A city fits the location slot with 1.0.
slot
The template field the candidate is meant to fill. “Stores” in the question calls for a location filter.
coherence(e, Q)
A 0-to-1 score for how well candidate e fits the rest of question Q. Paris, France has coherence 0.8.
Q
The whole question used as context for entity choice. “top 5 Paris stores by revenue.”
+
Add the weighted evidence pieces to get a candidate's score. 1.0 + 0.6 + 2.0 + 0.8 = 4.4 for Paris, France.

Read it left to right: start with spelling similarity, add the mention-based prior, add type fit, then add how well the candidate fits the rest of the question.

Give every possible “Paris” points for spelling, popularity, being the right kind of thing (a place, since the question says “stores”), and fitting the rest of the question. Convert points to probabilities with softmax, then only accept the winner if it is both confident (≥ τ) and clearly ahead of the runner-up (≥ δ).

Like a post office sorting a letter addressed to just “Paris”: the postcode (type), the sender’s usual destinations (prior) and the rest of the address (context) decide where it goes.

∝
Means “is proportional to”; normalize products across candidates to get probabilities. More supporting store records raise relative support.
P(e | m)
The candidate's probability from the mention before checking the database. Paris, France initially has 0.69 in the worked example.
·
Multiply the mention-based chance by the data-evidence factor. No stores in Paris, Texas drives its support toward zero.
P(stores exist at e)
A likelihood measuring whether relevant store records exist for entity e. The example has 12 stores in Paris, France and 0 in Paris, Texas.

If the store data held 12 stores in Paris, France and 0 in Paris, Texas, the Texas candidate would drop to about zero. If the reverse were true, the decision would flip. The data has the final say.

4.9Resolving several mentions jointly

When a question has several ambiguous mentions, resolving them one at a time can produce incoherent combinations. Resolve them together:

(ê₁, …, êⱼ) = argmax_{e₁…eⱼ} Σᵢ score(mᵢ, eᵢ) + Σ_{i<l} ψ(eᵢ, e_l)
(ê₁, …, ê_l)
The selected entity for each of l mentions; the hat marks the chosen entity and subscripts identify mention positions. (Paris TX, Austin TX) is one assignment.
argmax_{e₁…e_l}
Choose the combination of candidate entities with the highest combined score. Compare the two Paris/Austin pairings in the worked example.
e₁…e_l
Candidate entities for mentions 1 through l. e“ may be Paris and e” Austin.
Σ_i score(m_i, e_i)
Add each mention's individual score for its assigned candidate. Paris contributes 4.4 in France or 3.4 in Texas.
i, l
Indexes for mentions; l is the number of mentions. The example has l = 2: Paris and Austin.
m_i
The words for mention at position i. m_i can be “Paris.”
e_i
The candidate assigned to mention m_i. e_i can be Paris, Texas.
Σ_{i<l} ψ(e_i, e_l)
Add pairwise compatibility scores for entity pairs. Paris TX with Austin TX gets +1.5.
ψ(e_i, e_l)
A compatibility bonus or penalty for choosing two entities together. Two Texas cities receive a +1.5 same-state bonus.
+
Combine individual mention scores with pairwise compatibility. 3.4 + s_A + 1.5 = 4.9 + s_A for the Texas pair.

Read it left to right: add each mention's individual candidate score, then add bonuses or penalties for pairs of entities that fit together.

Here ψ(ei, el) is a pairwise compatibility bonus. For example, two cities in the same country or state are more likely to be mentioned together.

Worked example: “Compare Paris and Austin stores”

“Austin” resolves strongly to Austin, Texas; call its score s_A. For Paris, the individual scores are the same as in 4.8.

Compatibility: ψ(Paris TX, Austin TX) = +1.5 (same state), and ψ(Paris FR, Austin TX) = 0.

Joint assignmentIndividual scoresCompatibilityTotal
(Paris FR, Austin TX)4.4 + s_A04.4 + s_A
(Paris TX, Austin TX)3.4 + s_A1.54.9 + s_A

The joint decision flips to Paris, Texas. Comparing two Texas cities is more coherent.

How confident is it? Softmax over the two leading assignments (s_A cancels out):

P(Paris TX, Austin TX) = 1 / (1 + e^(−0.5)) ≈ 0.62 P(Paris FR, Austin TX) ≈ 0.38
P(Paris TX, Austin TX)
The softmax probability of the assignment with both cities in Texas. It is about 0.62.
1 / (1 + e^(−0.5))
The two-choice softmax formula for a score advantage of 0.5. It evaluates to about 0.62.
e
The mathematical constant about 2.718 used by the exponential function. e^(−0.5) is about 0.607.
−0.5
The negative score difference between the Texas pair (4.9) and the France pair (4.4). A 0.5 advantage yields probability about 0.62.
P(Paris FR, Austin TX)
The probability of the competing assignment with Paris in France. It is about 0.38; together the probabilities total 1.
≈
Means “approximately”; these values are rounded. 0.62 + 0.38 = 1.00.

The result is 0.62 ≥ 0.6 with a margin of 0.24 ≥ 0.2, so it is accepted, but only just. A borderline result like this should be stated as an assumption (“interpreting Paris as Paris, Texas”).

Two mentions are not independent. If someone says “Paris and Austin”, they are probably thinking of the same part of the world, so we reward pairs that belong together.

Solving a crossword: a word that was only your second guess for 3-across can become the right one because it fits the letters of 4-down.

Computational cost

With j mentions and K candidates each, there are Kj joint assignments. Ten mentions with 10 candidates each gives 1010 combinations, far too many to enumerate. Practical approaches:

MethodIdea
Beam searchKeep only the top-B partial assignments at each step
Loopy belief propagationPass “messages” between mentions until their beliefs stabilize
Coordinate ascentFix all mentions but one, improve it, then repeat

4.10Abstaining: the NIL option

Sometimes no candidate is right: the entity is new, misspelled beyond recognition, or simply not in the data. Forcing a match is worse than admitting uncertainty.

ê(m) = argmax_e score(m, e) if max_e score(m, e) ≥ τ_NIL NIL otherwise → ask, or report “not found”
ê(m)
The selected entity for mention m; the hat marks the chosen answer. For “Paris,” one candidate is Paris, France.
argmax_e score(m, e)
Choose the candidate e with the largest score for mention m. Paris, France scored 4.4 in the example.
max_e
The largest score among all candidates for this mention. That score must clear the NIL cutoff.
τ_NIL
The minimum score required to accept a real candidate; raising it makes the system abstain more often. If every candidate is below it, return NIL.
≥
Means “greater than or equal to.” A score exactly at τ_NIL is accepted.
NIL
A special “no suitable entity found” result, not a real candidate. Use it to ask the user or report “not found.”

An equivalent approach adds NIL as an explicit candidate with a fixed score sNIL. It wins whenever every real candidate scores below it.

A good librarian says “we don’t have that book” rather than handing you a different book with a similar title.

Calibration: does 70% confidence mean 70% correct?

A system is calibrated if, among all answers given with confidence p, a fraction of about p are actually correct. Measure this with the expected calibration error: group predictions into confidence bins B1…BM, then

ECE = Σ_m ( |B_m| / n ) · | accuracy(B_m) − confidence(B_m) |
ECE
Expected calibration error: the weighted average gap between confidence and actual accuracy across bins. A bin with 90% confidence but 70% accuracy has a 0.20 gap.
Σ_m
Add the error contribution from every confidence bin m. The lesson groups predictions into B₁…B_M.
m
The index of one confidence bin, not an entity mention here. One bin contains 100 links near 90% confidence.
B_m
The set of predictions in bin m. The example bin contains 100 links.
|B_m|
The number of predictions in bin m; bars mean the size of the set. |B_m| = 100 in the example.
n
The total number of predictions across all bins. The bin's weight is 100 divided by n.
accuracy(B_m)
The fraction of predictions in bin m that were correct, from 0 to 1. 70 correct out of 100 is 0.70.
confidence(B_m)
The average stated confidence in bin m, from 0 to 1. The example confidence is 0.90.
|…|
Absolute value: take the size of a difference regardless of its sign. |0.70 − 0.90| = 0.20.
·
Multiply bin error by its share of all predictions. The example contributes (100 / n) · 0.20.
M
The total number of confidence bins. The bins are B₁…B_M.

Example: 100 links made at about 90% confidence, of which only 70 were correct. That bin contributes |0.70 − 0.90| = 0.20 of error, weighted by its share of all links. The system is overconfident.

If a weather forecaster says “90% rain” on 100 days, it should rain on about 90 of them. If it rains on only 70, the forecaster is overconfident. ECE is the average size of that gap.

Fixing miscalibration: temperature scaling

Divide all scores by a learned temperature T before the softmax:

P(e | m) = softmax( score / T ) T > 1 → softer probabilities (fixes overconfidence) T < 1 → sharper probabilities (fixes underconfidence)
P(e | m)
The calibrated chance that entity e is right for mention m. The winning candidate's confidence is reported after softmax.
softmax
Converts candidate scores into probabilities summing to 1. Dividing scores by T does not change their ranking.
score
The candidate's numeric fit value before conversion to probability. Paris, France scored 4.4.
T
A learned positive temperature controlling how spread out probabilities are; higher values soften them and lower values sharpen them. T = 1 leaves scores unchanged.
/
Divide every candidate score by the same temperature before softmax. A higher T reduces score differences and confidence.
T > 1 / T < 1
Above 1 makes probabilities less extreme; below 1 makes them more extreme. Use T > 1 to reduce overconfidence.

The ranking doesn’t change, only the stated confidence, so the thresholds τ and δ become trustworthy.

4.11Putting it together: the parse score

A full parse of the question combines the intent, template slots, operators, time intervals and entity links:

log P(parse | Q) = log P(k̂ | Q) ← intent (4.2–4.3) + Σ_slots log P(slot filled correctly) ← template (4.4) + log P(op* | modifiers) ← aggregation (4.5) + log P(interval* | phrase, convention) ← time (4.6) + Σᵢ log P(êᵢ | mᵢ) ← entity links (4.8) + Σ_{i<l} ψ(êᵢ, ê_l) ← joint coherence (4.9)
log
Natural logarithm here: converts probabilities to log scores so multiplying probabilities becomes adding scores. The full interpretation adds its component log scores.
P(parse | Q)
The chance that a complete interpretation of question Q is correct. It combines intent, slots, time, and entity decisions.
parse
The complete structured interpretation of a question. Rank 5 stores by revenue in Paris last quarter.
Q
The original question text. “top 5 Paris stores by revenue last quarter.”
k̂
The selected intent; the hat marks the predicted intent. k̂ = rank for “top 5.”
log P(k̂ | Q)
The log probability of the chosen intent given the question. Section 4.3's worked example gives rank about 0.896.
Σ_slots
Add one log probability for each required template slot. The rank template needs measure, dimension, direction, N, and filters.

P(op* | modifiers)

interval*

Σ_i log P(ê_i | m_i)
Add log probabilities for each selected entity of each mention. The question includes the mention “Paris.”

The compatibility score for two selected entities together. Two Texas cities receive +1.5 in the worked example.

Read it left to right: score the intent, each filled slot, the operator, the time interval, every entity link, and finally how well the linked entities fit together.

The best parse is the argmax of this score. Each component with low confidence becomes a stated assumption or a clarifying question.

Adding logs is multiplying probabilities: the parse is only as trustworthy as its weakest part. One shaky piece (here, which Paris) is exactly what you flag to the user.

Check your understanding

  1. Compute: equal priors (0.5) for trend and rank. P(“over time” | trend) = 0.40, and P(“over time” | rank) = 0.05. What is P(trend | Q)?0.5 × 0.40 = 0.20 and 0.5 × 0.05 = 0.025. P(trend) = 0.20 / 0.225 ≈ 0.889. (Use the Quiz 1 button in the naive Bayes tool.)
  2. Compute: region A has 20 orders averaging $50, and region B has 80 orders averaging $100. What are the naive and correct overall averages?Naive: (50 + 100) / 2 = $75. Correct: (20 × 50 + 80 × 100) / 100 = (1,000 + 8,000) / 100 = $90.
  3. Compute: 500 orders came from 120 distinct customers. “How many customers bought?”120, using COUNT DISTINCT customer, not 500.
  4. Compute: two candidate entities score 3 and 1 (T = 1). What is the probability of the top one?1 / (1 + e−2) ≈ 0.881.
  5. Reason: why can resolving mentions jointly change an answer that looked confident in isolation?Pairwise compatibility ψ adds evidence that individual scoring can’t see. A candidate that is second-best alone can win when it fits much better with the other entities in the question.
  6. Apply: today is 2026-10-21. What interval is “last full month”?[2026-09-01, 2026-10-01), all of September 2026.

Key takeaways

  • Intent sets the answer’s shape. Use softmax for one intent, sigmoids for several, and cue-word likelihood ratios to show which words matter.
  • Each intent maps to a template with slots. Explain-type intents become decompositions of a change.
  • The aggregation operator comes from the user’s words. Count the right entity, and never average averages.
  • Time phrases need an anchor date and a calendar convention. Prefer complete periods for trends.
  • Entity linking = candidate generation + scoring (lexical, prior, type, context) + checking against the data.
  • Joint resolution with pairwise compatibility catches incoherent combinations, but borderline wins should be stated as assumptions.
  • NIL and calibration keep the system honest: “not found” beats a confident wrong match.
