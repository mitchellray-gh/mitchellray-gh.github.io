6.1The problem: many candidates, one answer

Module 5 produced a ranked list of candidate plans π₁, …, πₘ, each with a score. Executing them gives candidate answers A₁, …, Aₘ. This final module decides which answer to choose, when to clarify, whether to abstain, and how to present what was found.

Learning objectives

  1. Choose between candidate answers using expected utility, including uneven costs for different kinds of mistake.
  2. Treat the user's explicit requirements as hard constraints that come before any score.
  3. Raise confidence when independent derivations agree.
  4. Decide between answering, hedging and asking with the value of information.
  5. Set an answer threshold from a risk–coverage trade-off.
  6. Match the presentation format to the intent, and round numbers to their real precision.
  7. Trace one question end to end through all six modules.

The highest-scoring candidate is usually the right choice, but not always. A score measures probability, while the decision must also weigh consequences.

The most likely answer is not automatically the best answer. If a mistake is costly, a slightly less likely answer—or no answer yet—can be the wiser decision.

6.2Expected utility

Definition

Let w range over possible states of the world, such as which interpretation the user meant. Each candidate answer A has utility U(A,w): how good it is if the world is w.

EU(A) = Σw P(w | Q, evidence) · U(A, w) A* = argmaxA EU(A)

The simplest case: right or wrong

G is the gain from a correct answer. L is the loss from a wrong answer that someone acts on.

EU(A) = P(correct) · G − P(wrong) · L

In business settings, L is usually larger than G. A confident wrong number in a board deck costs more than a correct number gains.

Worked example

G = 1 and L = 2.

CandidateDescriptionP(correct)EU
A₁Certified monthly summary0.700.70(1) − 0.30(2) = +0.10
A₂Aggregated from raw transactions0.600.60(1) − 0.40(2) = −0.20

A₁ is the better choice. But EU = +0.10 is only slightly positive: with this loss ratio, even the best candidate is close to the point where answering stops being worthwhile.

Break-even confidence

Answering beats saying nothing (EU = 0) when:

P · G − (1 − P) · L > 0 ⟺ P > L / (G + L)
Loss ratio L : GBreak-even P
1 : 10.50
2 : 10.67
4 : 10.80
9 : 10.90

Lesson: the confidence threshold for answering isn't a fixed number. It follows from how costly a wrong answer would be. A high-stakes question needs more certainty.

Think of answering as placing a bet. Confidence is the chance the bet wins; gain and loss set the payoff. A high penalty for being wrong raises the probability you need before placing it.

6.3Hard constraints come first

Some requirements aren't trade-offs: they're conditions that any acceptable answer must meet. Every filter, threshold and grouping the user stated explicitly is a hard constraint κ.

Feasible set, then optimize

F = { A : A ⊨ κ for every explicit constraint κ in Q } A* = argmaxA ∈ F EU(A)

This is a lexicographic decision: satisfy the constraints first, and compare scores only within the feasible set. A higher score never buys back a violated constraint.

Worked example

Question: “Q3 revenue in EMEA, excluding returns.” Three constraints: κ₁ = Q3, κ₂ = EMEA, κ₃ = excludes returns.

CandidateScoreκ₁κ₂κ₃Feasible?
A₁ Gross revenue summary0.85✓✓✗ (includes returns)No
A₂ Net revenue summary0.72✓✓✓Yes
A₃ Transactions, returns filtered0.65✓✓✓Yes

A₂ is chosen. A₁ has the highest score but is removed, however convenient it looks.

When nothing is feasible

F = ∅ ⇒ do not relax constraints silently ⇒ report which constraint cannot be met, and why

For example, if no source tracks returns separately, say so. Don't quietly return gross revenue.

A user who stated a filter will assume it was applied. Silently dropping it gives an answer that looks right and is wrong, which is the most damaging kind of error.

6.4Agreement between independent derivations

If two independent plans reach the same answer, that agreement is strong evidence. Two wrong methods rarely produce the same wrong number.

Bayesian update

Let H = “answer A₁ is correct”.

P(H | agree) = P(agree | H) · P(H) ──────────────────────────────────────────────── P(agree | H) · P(H) + P(agree | ¬H) · P(¬H)

Worked example

Prior: P(A₁ correct) = 0.70. A₂, derived independently, is correct with probability 0.60. If A₂ is wrong it matches A₁ only by coincidence, with probability 0.05. If A₁ is wrong, A₂ matches it only by coincidence, with probability 0.05.

P(agree | H) = 0.60 + 0.40 × 0.05 = 0.62 P(agree | ¬H) = 0.05 P(H | agree) = 0.70 × 0.62 / (0.70 × 0.62 + 0.30 × 0.05) = 0.434 / (0.434 + 0.015) = 0.434 / 0.449 ≈ 0.97

Agreement raises confidence from 70% to about 97%.

Disagreement is also informative

If the two results differ, confidence in A₁ falls, and the size of the gap often points to the cause:

Pattern of gapLikely cause
Exact multiple (2×, 3×)Fan-out join (Module 3)
Small, steady % gapDifferent definition (gross vs. net) or exclusion rule
One period differsTime-window or partial-period mismatch (Module 4)
Opposite signRefunds or credits treated differently

Majority vote over several plans

With n plans, choose the answer most of them agree on:

A* = argmaxa Σj wj · 1[Aj = a] (wj = plan confidence)

This is called self-consistency. It is only valuable when the plans are genuinely independent: different sources or different paths. Five runs of the same plan agreeing proves nothing.

Agreement is not five independent votes if every result inherits the same join, source, or interpretation. Shared failure modes make repeated outputs look more certain than they are.

6.5Answer, hedge or ask? The value of information

When interpretations compete, there are three actions:

ActionWhat happens
AnswerCommit to the most likely interpretation silently
HedgeAnswer, stating the assumption so the user can correct it
AskClarify first, then answer correctly, at a cost c (time, friction)

Value of perfect information

EU(answer) = maxa Σw P(w) · U(a, w) ← best choice under uncertainty EU(perfect info) = Σw P(w) · maxa U(a, w) ← best choice if w were known VOI = EU(perfect info) − EU(answer) Ask ⟺ VOI > c

VOI is never negative: knowing more can't make the best decision worse.

Worked example: two interpretations

There are two interpretations, w₁ with P = 0.6 and w₂ with P = 0.4. Utilities: correct = +1, silently wrong = −2, wrong but assumption stated = −0.5. A stated assumption lets the user catch the error quickly, so the loss is smaller.

Answer (w₁, silently): 0.6(1) + 0.4(−2) = 0.6 − 0.8 = −0.20 Hedge (w₁, stated): 0.6(1) + 0.4(−0.5) = 0.6 − 0.2 = +0.40 Ask: 1 − c VOI (vs. silent answer) = 1 − (−0.20) = 1.20
Cost of asking cEU(ask)Best action
0.30.70Ask
0.60.40Tie between ask and hedge
0.80.20Hedge

Even a very cheap question doesn't make a silent answer worthwhile here. Silent answering is dominated by hedging at every cost.

Same setup, more confidence

With P(w₁) = 0.9:

Answer: 0.9 − 0.1(2) = 0.70 Hedge: 0.9 − 0.1(0.5) = 0.85 Ask: 1 − c = 0.70 at c = 0.3

Hedge wins: interruptions aren't worth it when one interpretation is strongly favoured, but the assumption is still worth stating.

Decision summary

high confidence, low stakes → answer, briefly noting any assumption moderate confidence or high stakes → hedge: answer plus stated assumptions and alternatives low confidence and high stakes → ask

A hedge is not an apology. It is an immediate useful answer with the uncertain assumption made visible. Asking has value when the improvement is worth the interruption.

6.6Abstention: the risk–coverage trade-off

Across many questions, an answer threshold τ trades coverage against risk:

Coverage(τ) = fraction of questions answered (Conf ≥ τ) Risk(τ) = error rate among the answered questions

Worked example: 100 questions

Threshold τAnsweredWrongCoverageRisk
0.010020100%20.0%
0.5801080%12.5%
0.760360%5.0%
0.930130%3.3%

Goal: keep risk ≤ 5%. The lowest threshold that meets the target is τ = 0.7, which answers 60% of questions.

τ* = min { τ : Risk(τ) ≤ target } ← maximize coverage subject to a risk budget

Why calibration matters here

This curve only works if the confidence is calibrated (Module 4, section 4.10). If “0.8” really means “60% correct”, any threshold gives far more errors than expected.

Abstaining well

“I don't know” isn't the only alternative to an answer. Partial answers are often better:

SituationBetter than a flat refusal
One constraint can't be metAnswer the rest, and explain the gap
Two interpretations are equally likelyShow both, clearly labelled
Data is incomplete for the latest periodAnswer for complete periods, and flag the partial one

Choose the lowest threshold that satisfies the risk budget. That preserves as much useful coverage as the target allows.

Answer first

Readers value the beginning of an answer most. Order the content by value: direct answer → key evidence → assumptions → detail.

Round to the real precision

Don't report more digits than the uncertainty supports. With relative uncertainty ε:

meaningful significant figures ≈ ⌈−log₁₀ ε⌉ + 1

Reporting “$1,047,382” when the answer is only good to ±5% implies a precision that isn't there.

Make assumptions visible

Every latent variable inferred in Modules 3–4 (time window, calendar, entity choice, default exclusions) whose confidence was below about 0.9 should appear as a stated assumption. That is what makes the hedge in 6.5 work: it turns a −2 into a −0.5.

A good answer is like a well-labelled chart: lead the eye to the conclusion, then expose the evidence and the assumptions that let someone check it.

Key takeaways

  • Expected utility, not raw score, picks the answer, and the threshold for answering follows from the cost of a wrong answer.
  • The user's explicit requirements are hard constraints. Filter first, optimize second, and never relax them silently.
  • Independent agreement sharply raises confidence, and the pattern of a disagreement points to its cause.
  • Answer, hedge or ask is a value-of-information decision. Hedging is often the best middle ground.
  • Thresholds come from a risk–coverage target and only work with calibrated confidence.
  • Presentation follows intent: lead with the answer, round to the real precision, and state assumptions.
