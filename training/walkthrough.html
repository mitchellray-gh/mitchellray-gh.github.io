1. Relevance and source selection

What goes in: The question, candidate source descriptions and schemas, text matches, semantic similarity, concept coverage, and each source’s trust prior.

rel(d) = 0.5 · cos + 0.2 · BM25_norm + 0.3 · coverage P(d | Q) ∝ rel(d) · P(d)
rel(d)
Combined relevance of source d to the question. For d_2, regional_revenue_summary, the worked score is 0.905.
cos
Cosine similarity: semantic resemblance between question and source. The worked d_2 value is 0.85.
BM25_norm
Normalized exact-word match score; BM25 rewards informative shared terms. The worked d_2 value is 0.90.
coverage
Fraction of requested concepts a source covers. d_2 covers Revenue, Month, and Region: 3/3 = 1.000.
0.5, 0.2, 0.3
Weights for cosine, BM25, and concept coverage in this worked example.
P(d)
Prior trust in source d, before using the question. The certified d_2 prior is 0.5 in the worked example.
P(d | Q)
Normalized source probability after relevance and prior are combined. The worked posteriors are d_2: about 0.66, d_1: about 0.28, and d_3: about 0.06.
∝
Proportional to: multiply the fit by its prior, then normalize across candidate sources.

Handoff

Choose d_2, regional_revenue_summary (certified). Its posterior is about 0.66; orders_fact (d_1) remains a credible backup, while employee_directory (d_3) is a weak match.

Why: Cosine catches meaning, BM25 checks the words, and concept coverage checks business requirements. The prior lets certification and usage history influence the winner instead of treating every source as equally trustworthy.

2. Lexicon and ontology mapping

What goes in: The question’s surface words, the candidate source concepts, and the business lexicon and ontology.

sales

Maps to the Revenue concept; the lesson lists sales, turnover, and income from sales as Revenue forms.

monthly

Maps to Month as the time grain.

by region

Maps to Region as the grouping concept.

Handoff

Concept set: {Revenue, Month, Region}. The candidate source d_2 explicitly has Revenue, Month, and Region fields.

Why: Different words can name the same business concept, so mapping sales to Revenue connects the user’s language to the data model. Keeping concepts distinct also avoids treating a related measure, such as cost, as if it were sales.

Lesson 2: Concept mapping

3. Schema graph and join paths

What goes in: The concept set, tables and columns, candidate join edges, and evidence about how their values overlap.

contain(x ⊆ y) = shared distinct values / distinct values in x Orders.cust_id to Customers.customer_id: 940 / 950 ≈ 0.989
contain(x ⊆ y)
Directional containment: the fraction of distinct values in x that also appear in y. A high score supports x as a foreign key into y.
x
The column being checked. Orders.cust_id has 950 distinct values in the lesson example.
y
The possible referenced column. Customers.customer_id has 1,000 distinct values.
940
Number of values shared by the two columns in the lesson example.
940 / 950 ≈ 0.989
Containment score: nearly all Orders customer IDs occur in Customers. The lesson also reports Jaccard similarity 0.931.

Handoff

Primary route: d_2 is a single monthly regional summary, so no join is needed. Fallback route: the schema graph contains Regions → Customers → Orders; the high containment evidence supports the Orders-to-Customers link if the raw-orders backup is used.

Why: A graph makes candidate join paths explicit instead of guessing from table names. Directional containment helps distinguish a plausible foreign-key link from a mere overlap; the primary source can avoid joins altogether.

Lesson 3: Schema graphs and join paths

4. Intent and entity parsing

What goes in: The original words plus the mapped concepts and available source structure.

Handoff

Parse: intent = aggregate; measure = Revenue; group dimensions = Region and Month; time grain = Month; explicit date window = none; explicit filters = none.

Why: “By region” asks for grouped values, and “monthly” supplies a time grain; together these describe an aggregate result rather than a top-N ranking. No region value or bounded time range was named, so the parser must not invent one.

Lesson 4: Intent and entity parsing

5. Plan and slot filling

What goes in: The parsed intent, requested measure and dimensions, candidate source d_2, and the available fields.

Handoff

Plan: aggregate Revenue for each Month and Region using d_2.Revenue, d_2.Month, and d_2.Region. Apply no unstated filters; return grouped results for the available months.

The module’s worked slot-matching example shows why a global assignment can beat a greedy pick: Revenue-to-amount scores 0.90 and Revenue-to-sales_total scores 0.80; the best full assignment totals 1.65 rather than the greedy 1.00. Those are that lesson’s illustration scores, not invented confidence values for d_2; here the summary’s named fields fit the requested slots directly.

Why: Slot filling turns an abstract request into field choices and an executable shape. Ranking complete plans avoids choosing one attractive field match that leaves another slot with a worse assignment.

Lesson 5: Planning, slot filling, and ranking

6. Decision under uncertainty

What goes in: Ranked plans and candidate answers, confidence in each answer, the cost of a wrong answer, and whether asking would resolve a consequential ambiguity.

Handoff

The course’s worked decision comparison uses gain G = 1 and loss L = 2: the certified monthly-summary answer A_1 has P(correct) = 0.70 and expected utility +0.10; the raw-transaction answer A_2 has P(correct) = 0.60 and expected utility -0.20. Select the certified-summary path, but treat its small positive margin cautiously.

Decision for this question: Answer with a stated assumption that “sales” means Revenue, using d_2. Since no period is specified, say that results cover the available months. If the user needs a particular date range or gross-versus-net treatment and that choice matters, ask before presenting a definitive figure; otherwise hedge by making the assumption visible.

Why: The highest-confidence candidate is not automatically safe when errors are costly. Here the certified summary beats the raw aggregation in the lesson’s own example, but the narrow utility margin supports disclosing the interpretation and asking when the missing detail could change the decision.

Lesson 6: Decision under uncertainty

Whole picture

The question becomes a ranked source, then business concepts, a justified data route, a structured intent, and a field-filled plan. The final decision uses evidence and the cost of being wrong to choose whether to answer, hedge, or ask.
